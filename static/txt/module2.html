--- label: 2.1 title: Energy Demand --- label: 2.1.1 title: Why is Energy Needed? content: It’s no secret that data centers use a TON of electricity. The reason for the massive power bill is because of both the servers running (which is what runs things like ChatGPT) and cooling systems for the aforementioned servers.

How many homes do you think could be powered by one large data center? --- label: 2.1.2 title: How Much Energy is Needed? content:
In previous years, data centers generally required somewhere between 5 and 10 kW of energy per rack with 10kW being around the same amount of energy taken by a suburban home with all major appliances running at once.

Previously, a single data center would house a few hundred to a few thousand racks. Nowadays, higher end racks consume 50-100kW, with a single large facility possibly housing over 100,000 racks. This means that a single one of these buildings could be using up as much energy as a million homes just for servers. This doesn’t even account for the amount of energy required to cool all of the servers!
This was a 2024 estimate for how much energy would be consumed by data centers after 2024. It underestimated the growth of the industry. In 2025, a reported ~450TWh of energy was consumed, closer to the prediction for the year 2027.
--- label: 2.1.3 title: Where Does the Energy Come From? content: Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is how we are able to tell how efficiently a data center is using energy. It is calculated by dividing the total energy cost of a data center by the energy cost of IT equipment (such as servers). The best possible PUE a data center can have is 1.0 (the lower the better), but most data centers average ~1.5 PUE or, in special cases, 1.2 PUE for very efficient data centers. What this means is that if a data center has a PUE score of 1.5 and its servers alone consume the same amount of power as 100,000 homes, the entire building is actually using the same amount of power as 150,000 homes. In fact, Meta currently has plans for a data center, and when finished, it will use twice as much energy as the entire city of New Orleans! Another data center planned in Wyoming will use more power than every home in the state combined. --- label: 2.1.4 title: Understanding Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) content: In 2025, the energy sources for data centers were as follows:
  • 30% coal
  • 27% renewable energy (solar, water, wind)
  • 26% natural gas
  • 15% nuclear
Because a significant amount of this energy comes from non-renewable sources, data centers are having a strong impact on CO2 emissions. --- label: 2.2 title: Water Consumption --- label: 2.2.1 title: Heat as a Byproduct content: When data center servers are up and running, they generate a lot of heat. Electrical energy is used to store and manipulate data in a data center, and inevitably, some of it will be lost to the surrounding air as heat. This creates a problem: too much heat for too long damages the hardware, and that interrupts operations. The solution is to actively cool down the spaces that the servers stay in. In the past, air circulation by fans and air conditioning has been sufficient. However, in recent years a new method of cooling has been preferred. --- label: 2.2.2 title: What Role Does Cold Water Play? content: Currently, one of the emerging methods of cooling is what is known as “evaporative cooling”. This method uses evaporating water to cool down the hot hardware. Evaporative cooling is more energy efficient than air cooling. However, this method uses a lot of water, which is lost during the cooling process. The water used for cooling data centers comes from local sources of freshwater, meaning that clean water is being redirected from consumers to corporations who operate data centers. --- label: 2.2.3 title: How Much Water is Needed? content: Similarly to Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), there is a metric for water usage, Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE). This is important for data center info because it tells us how much water is being spent on facilities other than IT equipment. The formula to calculate it is: Annual water usage / Annual IT equipment energy usage (kWh) We express WUE in Liters/kWh, meaning that we calculate how many liters of water are used per kilowatt hour of energy used by the IT equipment. A score of 0 is the (unrealistic) goal. --- label: 2.2.4 title: Where Does the Water Come From? content: Water is the primary resource used for cooling data center servers because it carries heat away more efficiently than air. Most of the time, they tap into freshwater resources because special equipment is required to handle saltwater. Unfortunately, only 3% of all water in the world is freshwater (with 0.5% being drinkable water for humans), meaning that a majority of water used by data centers makes water less accessible for people. --- label: 2.3 title: Labor in Other Countries --- label: 2.3.1 title: Pricing (RAM) content: One of the cost effects of data centers (especially AI data centers) is that the price of RAM has increased. RAM is a computer’s short term memory, allowing devices to run multiple applications at once and allowing for better overall performance. Without RAM, modern day computer systems would not work. RAM prices have increased by 90% in the first quarter of 2026 over the fourth quarter of 2025. AI data centers require a lot more RAM than any personal device, causing this massive surge in pricing. One specific unit, the DDR5-6000, has increased from just over $100 in early 2025 to $500 in 2026. This is a graph showing the increase in price of the DDR5-6000 2x16GB. It goes from around $100 in November 2024 to over $500 in March 2026. --- label: 2.3.2 title: Rare Metals content: Certain components of data centers need specific metals in order to operate. These metals are rare, and mining operations have a few complications. Firstly, mines for these metals are often in countries where laborers experience unsafe working conditions. Mine collapses, landslides, and other dangerous events threaten the lives of workers. In addition, mining operations of this variety have negative impacts on wildlife. Many highly impactful or “keystone” species are affected by these mines. Finally, rare metal mining impacts groundwater, which means that the drinking water that 50% of the US relies on is threatened if mining operations expand domestically. ---
